1 Purpose
The project is an opportunity to use statistical learning to answer a consequential, clearly defined question. A
successful project connects a decision or scientific question to a carefully curated dataset, compares reasonable
approaches, evaluates them appropriately, and communicates what the evidence does and does not support.
The project should demonstrate the course learning outcomes:
1. connect a real question to a mathematical or statistical formulation;
2. describe and curate data suitable for that formulation;
3. use and compare tools taught in the course or beyond; you should be able to justify and defend your approach;
4. evaluate generalisation, uncertainty, assumptions, and limitations; and
5. translate the analysis into an appropriate recommendation or scientific conclusion.
2 Team rules
• Ateam may consist of at most four members; teams will be assigned.
• Anindividual project is allowed if approved by the instructor after project proposal submission.
• Every member must make a substantive contribution to question formulation, data work, analysis, and
communication.
• Every member must speak during the presentation and may be asked any question during the defence.
• Normally, members receive the same report score. Presentation and defence scores may differ when individual
understanding or contribution is demonstrably uneven.

3 What the project must contain
3.1 A proper question
State one primary question in a single sentence. A useful template is:
For [decision maker or scientific audience], can [information available at the decision time] help
[predict, compare, segment, recommend, or prioritise] [well-defined outcome] for [population,
place, and period]?
The question must be important enough that an answer would influence a decision, resource allocation, scientific
understanding, or public discussion. It must also be narrow enough to answer within the course timeline.
Do not begin with “we will apply random forests.” Begin with the question. Methods should follow from the
estimand, prediction target, decision, and data structure.
Use causal language such as “effect,” “impact,” or “causes” only when the sampling and identification strategy
support it. Most course projects will establish prediction or association, not causation.
3.2 Dataset
Teams must construct an analysis-ready dataset rather than merely download a file and fit a model. Curation may
include combining sources, reshaping records into the correct observational unit, creating new variables, resolving
identifiers, documenting missing elements, or defining a prediction time.
Required data documentation:
• original source, access date, licence or terms of use, and a persistent link;
• unit of observation and target population;
• inclusion and exclusion criteria;
• data dictionary for variables used in the analysis;
• all joins, filters, recodes, aggregations, and derived variables;
• missing-data, duplicate, outlier, and measurement-error checks;
• afrozen raw-data snapshot or a script that recreates it;
• limitations in coverage, sampling, proxies, and label quality; and
• confirmation that no confidential or personally identifying information is exposed.
If an API or continuously updated source is used, save the retrieval date, query, parameters, response metadata,
and an immutable analysis snapshot. If data cannot legally be redistributed, provide a retrieval script and clear
instructions instead.
3.3 A purposeful variety of tools
The analysis must include all of the following:
1. Exploratory analysis: distributions, missingness, dependence, class balance, subgroups, and informative
graphics.
2. A transparent baseline: for example, a mean or prevalence rule, a simple linear/logistic model, or a
popularity recommender.
3. At least two statistical-learning approaches: preferably from different course families, such as regularised
regression versus random forest, multinomial logit versus a tree model, or PCA plus clustering versus clustering
on raw features. It can of course include tools/techniques not taught in class as well.
4. Out-of-sample evaluation: a split or resampling scheme that matches how the model would be used.
5. Interpretation and diagnosis: uncertainty, residuals or errors, sensitivity checks, important failure cases,
and limitations.
Using many algorithms without a reason will not earn extra credit. Each method must have a role, which can be:
baseline, interpretation, benchmark, dimension reduction, segmentation, recommendation, survival modelling, etc.

4 Validation rules
• Define the outcome and evaluation metric before comparing models.
• Keep a final test set untouched until the analysis plan is stable.
• Split by time for future prediction, by person for repeated observations, and by location or institution when
deployment is to a new group.
• Perform preprocessing, feature selection, imputation, and tuning using training data only.
• Compare against a simple baseline and report the uncertainty or variability of performance.
• For imbalanced outcomes, do not rely on accuracy alone; consider precision, recall, AUC, ROC-AUC, etc.
• For regression, report an interpretable error scale in addition to any dimensionless metric.
• For recommenders, separate users or interactions correctly and report coverage or popularity bias as well as
prediction accuracy.
• For survival outcomes, respect censoring and avoid treating censored times as observed event times.
• For multiple testing, report the family of hypotheses and control a suitable error rate such as FDR.
• Report meaningful negative results. Do not select only the most favourable metric, subgroup, or split.
5 Use of generative AI
AI tools may be used to brainstorm code, debug, improve prose, or prototype a visual, subject to course and
university policy. They do not replace statistical judgement.
If AI is used:
• retain an AI_USE.md log naming the tool, date, purpose, and representative prompts;
• identify material AI-generated code, text, or graphics and explain how it was checked;
• test all generated code and verify every statistical claim and citation;
• do not upload confidential, licensed, or personal data to an unauthorised service;
• do not fabricate observations, citations, results, or a defence answer; and
• be prepared to explain and modify every submitted line of analysis.
Example of an acceptable prompt:
“Using this data dictionary and a pre-registered temporal split, propose three ggplot designs that compare
calibration across groups. Do not calculate results. Explain what each visual would reveal and provide
R code using only columns listed here.”
The team remains fully responsible for the submitted work, including mistakes introduced by an AI system.
6 Required deliverables
6.1 Project proposal
A one-page proposal is a required approval checkpoint. It should contain:
• team members and provisional title;
• primary question and why it matters;
• intended decision maker or scientific audience;
• proposed sources and evidence that the data are accessible;
• unit of observation, expected outcome, and approximate sample size;
• two or three candidate methods and the proposed validation design;
• the largest anticipated risk and a fallback plan; and
• ashort division of responsibilities.
The proposal carries 5 points and full score is given if all the checkpoints above are addressed, and the project is
approved by the instructor. The project must be approved before substantial work begins.

Large raw files need not be submitted if the retrieval instructions are reliable.
Note that the document mentions R, but you can use any software of choice.
6.3 Final report (and project analysis package)— 10 points
The main text of the report should be at most six A4 pages in 11- or 12-point font, excluding references and a
concise technical appendix. The appendix is supporting material and is not a way to move essential reasoning out
of the graded report.
Required sections:
1. Abstract: the question, data, principal method, and main finding.
2. Problem and relevance: decision or scientific context; precise target.
3. Data curation: provenance, unit, construction, quality, and limitations.
4. Methods and validation: baseline, competing tools, assumptions, metrics, and split design.
5. Results: out-of-sample comparisons, uncertainty, diagnostics, and clear figures.
6. Discussion: answer to the question, practical meaning, failures, ethics, and next step.
7. Reproducibility and contributions: code/data location and each member’s contribution.
8. References and AI-use disclosure.
6.4 Presentation and defence — 15 points
Unless the instructor announces a different schedule, plan for a 12–15-minute presentation followed by
approximately 5–7 minutes of questions and defence.
The presentation should:
• motivate the question and name the audience within the first minute;
• show how raw records became the analysis dataset;
• justify the baseline, methods, validation scheme, and metrics;
• focus on two or three decisive results rather than every model tried;
• include readable, self-explanatory figures with units and uncertainty;
• present one important model failure or limitation; and
• finish with a direct answer and an appropriate action or next analysis.
During the defence, the team should be able to explain sampling, leakage risks, variable availability, assumptions,
model choices, tuning, uncertainty, fairness or ethics, and why the conclusion would change under plausible alternative
decisions.
